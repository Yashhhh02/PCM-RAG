### 1. Atomic mass (relative to \(^{12}\)C)  
From page 25: *“The atomic mass of an element is expressed relative to \(^{12}\)C isotope of carbon, which has an exact value of 12 u.”*  
If \(m_{\text{atom}}\) is the mass of an atom of the element and \(m_{^{12}\text{C}}=12\;\text{u}\) is the mass of one \(^{12}\)C atom, then  

\[
A \;=\;\frac{m_{\text{atom}}}{m_{^{12}\text{C}}}\times 12\;\text{u}
      \;=\;\frac{m_{\text{atom}}}{12\;\text{u}}\times 12\;\text{u}
      \;=\;m_{\text{atom}}\;(\text{in u})
\]

Thus the atomic mass in unified mass units (u) is obtained by scaling the atom’s mass to the \(^{12}\)C reference. (p. 25)

---

### 2. Molecular (or molar) mass of a molecule  
From page 25: *“The molecular mass of a molecule is obtained by taking the sum of the atomic masses of different atoms present in a molecule.”*  
If a molecule contains \(n_i\) atoms of element \(i\) whose atomic mass is \(A_i\), then  

\[
M_{\text{molecule}} \;=\;\sum_i n_i\,A_i
\]

For a substance expressed per mole, the same sum gives the molar mass \(M\) (g mol\(^{-1}\)). (p. 25)

---

### 3. Amount of substance (moles) and Avogadro’s constant  
From page 25: *“The number of atoms, molecules or any other particles … are expressed in the terms of Avogadro constant (6.022 × 10\(^{23}\)). This is known as 1 mol of the respective particles.”*  
Let \(N\) be the number of entities and \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\;\text{mol}^{-1}\). Then  

\[
n \;=\;\frac{N}{N_A}\qquad\text{or}\qquad N \;=\;n\,N_A
\]

where \(n\) is the amount of substance in moles. (p. 25)

---

### 4. Mass percent (w/w %) of a solute in a solution  
From page 21: *“Mass per cent or weight per cent (w/w %)”* is listed as one of the ways to express concentration.  
By definition,  

\[
\boxed{\%\,w/w \;=\;\frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solution}}}\times 100}
\]

where \(m_{\text{solute}}\) and \(m_{\text{solution}}\) are the masses of solute and total solution, respectively. (p. 21)

---

### 5. Mole fraction of a component  
From page 21: *“Mole fraction”* is another concentration unit.  
For a component \(i\) in a mixture,  

\[
\boxed{X_i \;=\;\frac{n_i}{\displaystyle\sum_j n_j}}
\]

with \(n_i\) the number of moles of component \(i\) and the sum over all components. (p. 21)

---

### 6. Molarity (M)  
From page 21: *“Molarity”* is defined as moles of solute per litre of solution.  

\[
\boxed{M \;=\;\frac{n_{\text{solute}}\;(\text{mol})}{V_{\text{solution}}\;(\text{L})}}
\]

\(V_{\text{solution}}\) must be expressed in litres. (p. 21)

---

### 7. Molality (b or m)  
From page 21: *“Molality”* is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent.  

\[
\boxed{b \;=\;\frac{n_{\text{solute}}\;(\text{mol})}{m_{\text{solvent}}\;(\text{kg})}}
\]

\(m_{\text{solvent}}\) is the mass of the solvent only. (p. 21)

---

### 8. Limiting‑reagent relationship (from stoichiometry)  
From page 21: *“The reactant which is present in the least amount gets consumed after sometime … Hence, the reactant … limits the amount of product formed and is … called the limiting reagent.”*  
For a balanced reaction  

\[
\sum_i \nu_i \,\text{R}_i \;\longrightarrow\; \sum_k \nu_k \,\text{P}_k
\]

where \(\nu_i\) and \(\nu_k\) are the stoichiometric coefficients (positive for reactants, negative for products in the usual sign convention), the maximum extent of reaction \(\xi_{\max}\) is set by the reactant that runs out first:

\[
\xi_{\max} \;=\;\min_i\left(\frac{n_i}{\nu_i}\right)
\]

The amount (in moles) of any product \(k\) that can be formed is then  

\[
n_{P_k} \;=\;\nu_k\,\xi_{\max}
\]

Thus the limiting reagent determines the theoretical yield. (p. 21)

**Q: What are the important differences between concepts in basic-concepts?**

**A:** - **Precision vs. Accuracy** – Precision refers to how close repeated measurements are to each other; accuracy refers to how close a measurement is to the true value (Page 13).  
- **Mass percent, Mole fraction, Molarity, Molality** – These are different ways to express the amount of solute in a solution (Page 21):  
  * Mass percent (w/w %) = (mass of solute / total mass of solution) × 100.  
  * Mole fraction = (moles of component) / (total moles of all components).  
  * Molarity = (moles of solute) / (volume of solution in litres).  
  * Molality = (moles of solute) / (mass of solvent in kilograms).  
- **Limiting reagent vs. Excess reagent** – The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely consumed first and thus determines the amount of product formed; any other reactant present in greater amount remains as the excess reagent (Page 21).  
- **Laws of chemical combination** – They differ in what they relate:  
  * Law of Conservation of Mass – mass is unchanged in a chemical reaction.  
  * Law of Definite Proportions – a pure compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass.  
  * Law of Multiple Proportions – when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole‑number ratios.  
  * Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes – gases react with each other in volumes that bear simple whole‑number ratios (at same temperature and pressure).  
  * Avogadro’s Law – equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules (Page 25).  
- **Atomic mass vs. Molecular mass** – Atomic mass is the relative mass of an atom based on the ¹²C isotope (= 12 u); molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule (Page 25).  
- **Avogadro constant vs. Mole** – The Avogadro constant (6.022 × 10²³) is the number of entities (atoms, molecules, etc.) in one mole; a mole is the amount of substance that contains exactly that many entities (Page 25).  
- **SI units vs. Other systems** – SI (International System of Units) is the universally agreed uniform system; measurements in other systems can be interconverted using dimensional analysis (Page 25).  
- **Scientific notation vs. Regular decimal notation** – Scientific notation expresses very large or small numbers (range 10⁻³¹ to 10²³) as a coefficient times a power of ten, making them easier to handle (Page 25).  
- **Significant‑figure rules for addition/subtraction vs. multiplication/division** – In addition/subtraction the result is limited by the least number of decimal places; in multiplication/division the result is limited by the measurement with the fewest significant figures (Page 13).

**Q: What are the limitations of the laws in chemical-bonding?**

**A:** The limitations of the octet rule (often referred to as the “law” of chemical bonding in this context) are discussed in the notes as follows:

- **Incomplete octet** – In some compounds the central atom is surrounded by fewer than eight electrons, especially for elements having less than four valence electrons (e.g., Li, Be, B)【6†L13-L20】.  
- **Odd‑electron molecules** – Species with an odd number of electrons (e.g., NO, NO₂) do not satisfy the octet for all atoms【6†L21-L26】.  
- **Expanded octet** – Elements in and beyond the third period can accommodate more than eight valence electrons by using available d orbitals (e.g., PF₅, SF₆)【6†L27-L33】.  
- **Based on noble‑gas inertness** – The rule assumes bonding follows the inertness of noble gases, yet some noble gases (e.g., Xe, Kr) do form compounds (XeF₂, etc.)【7†L5-L9】.  
- **No account of molecular shape** – The octet rule does not explain the geometry of molecules【7†L9-L10】.  
- **Silent on energetic stability** – It gives no information about the relative stability of molecules or the energy involved in bonding【7†L10-L12】.  

These points collectively constitute the limitations of the octet rule as presented in the chemical‑bonding chapter.【6†L13-L33】【7†L5-L12】

**Q: Explain the physical significance of the concepts in chemical-bonding.**

**A:** The physical significance of the concepts discussed in the chemical‑bonding chapter can be understood as follows:

- **VSEPR model** – It predicts the geometrical shape of a molecule by assuming that electron‑pair repulsions (lone‑pair–lone‑pair > lone‑pair–bond‑pair > bond‑pair–bond‑pair) keep the pairs as far apart as possible. This gives a direct link between the distribution of electron pairs and the observed molecular geometry (p.34).  

- **Valence‑bond (VB) approach** – It focuses on the energetics of bond formation: when two atoms approach, the overlap of their singly occupied orbitals lowers the potential energy of the system. At the equilibrium internuclear distance the energy reaches a minimum; further approach raises the energy sharply, leading to destabilisation. Thus orbital overlap is the physical origin of the attractive force that holds atoms together (p.34).  

- **Hybridisation** – By mixing s, p and/or d orbitals, hybrid orbitals are formed that have the correct directional properties to explain the observed bond angles and molecular shapes (e.g., sp, sp², sp³ for linear, trigonal‑planar, tetrahedral geometries). This concept bridges the gap between simple orbital overlap and the actual geometry of molecules (p.34).  

- **Molecular‑orbital (MO) theory** – Bonding results from the linear combination of atomic orbitals to form molecular orbitals. The number of MOs equals the number of combining AOs. Bonding MOs increase electron density between the nuclei, reducing nuclear repulsion and lowering the energy relative to the parent AOs; antibonding MOs have a nodal plane of zero electron density between the nuclei, increasing repulsion and raising the energy. The overall stability of a molecule depends on having more electrons in bonding than in antibonding MOs (p.34, p.28).  

- **Conditions for orbital combination** – Effective combination requires (1) the AOs to have similar energies, (2) the same symmetry about the molecular axis, and (3) maximum overlap. Only when these are satisfied do bonding and antibonding MOs form with the described energy changes (p.28).  

- **Types of MOs (σ, π, δ)** – Sigma (σ) orbitals have electron density along the internuclear axis, pi (π) orbitals have side‑on overlap, and delta (δ) orbitals involve more complex overlap. These classifications reflect how the electron density is distributed in space and consequently influence bond strength and directionality (p.28).  

- **Hydrogen bonding** – When a hydrogen atom is situated between two highly electronegative atoms, the resulting H‑bond introduces a strong electrostatic attraction that significantly affects the structure (e.g., chain or network formation) and properties (e.g., boiling point, solubility) of compounds. The effect is greatest in the solid state and least in the gaseous state (p.33).  

Together, these concepts explain how atomic interactions lead to the observed shapes, energies, and physical behaviour of chemical substances, each providing a different but complementary view of the same underlying physical reality.   (p.1, p.34, p.28, p.33)

**Q: What are the important differences between concepts in chemical-bonding?**

**A:** The important differences among the concepts discussed in the chemical‑bonding chapter are:

- **Lewis/Kossel approach** – explains bond formation through the attainment of noble‑gas configurations either by electron transfer (ionic/electrovalent bond) or by sharing of electron pairs (covalent bond); uses Lewis dot symbols to show valence electrons and bonding/lone pairs. (p. 33, p. 1)

- **VSEPR model** – predicts molecular geometry solely from repulsions between electron pairs (lone‑pair/lone‑pair > lone‑pair/bonding‑pair > bonding‑pair/bonding‑pair); the arrangement that minimizes these repulsions gives the observed shape. (p. 34)

- **Valence‑Bond (VB) theory** – focuses on the energetics of bond formation; a bond results from the overlap of atomic orbitals (σ bond from end‑to‑end/head‑on overlap, π bond from sidewise overlap); explains bond strength and directionality, and introduces hybridisation to account for observed geometries. (p. 34, p. 21)

- **Molecular‑Orbital (MO) theory** – combines atomic orbitals to form molecular orbitals; the number of MOs equals the number of AOs; bonding MOs increase electron density between nuclei and are lower in energy, while antibonding MOs have a nodal plane (zero density) and higher energy; electrons fill MOs following Aufbau, Pauli and Hund’s rules; stability requires more electrons in bonding than in antibonding orbitals. (p. 34)

- **Hybridisation** – mixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies to produce an equal number of equivalent hybrid orbitals (e.g., one s + three p → four sp³); these hybrids are used in bond formation to explain molecular shapes such as tetrahedral, trigonal planar, etc. (p. 21, p. 34)

- **Hydrogen bond** – forms when a hydrogen atom is situated between two highly electronegative atoms (e.g., F, O, N); can be inter‑molecular (between different molecules) or intra‑molecular (within the same molecule); exerts a strong influence on the structure and properties of many compounds. (p. 33, p. 34)

- **σ vs. π bonds** – σ bonds arise from end‑to‑end overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis; π bonds arise from sidewise overlap with orbitals parallel to each other and perpendicular to the axis; σ bonds are stronger because of greater overlap. (p. 21)

- **Resonance** – when a single Lewis structure cannot adequately represent a molecule, multiple contributing structures (canonical forms) are written; their combination gives the resonance hybrid, which reflects delocalisation of electrons. (p. 33)

- **Bond parameters** – bond length, bond angle, bond enthalpy, bond order and bond polarity are key features that significantly affect the physical and chemical properties of compounds. (p. 33)

These points highlight how each theory or concept addresses different aspects of chemical bonding—electron counting, geometry, orbital overlap, energy levels, and intermolecular forces—according to the information provided in the notes.

**Q: What are the important differences between concepts in classification-of-elements?**

**A:** The classification‑of‑elements unit distinguishes several key concepts, and the important differences among them are:

- **Block classification (s, p, d, f)** – Elements are grouped into four types according to the orbital that is being filled (s‑block, p‑block, d‑block, f‑block) [p. 22].  
  - The **s‑block** specifically comprises Group 1 (alkali metals) and Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) whose outermost electronic configuration is \(ns^{1}\) or \(ns^{2}\) [p. 9].  
  - The other blocks are defined only by their electronic‑configuration basis (p, d, f orbitals) [p. 22].

- **Periodic trends across a period versus down a group** –  
  - **Atomic radii**: decrease from left to right in a period; increase with atomic number down a group [p. 22].  
  - **Ionization enthalpy**: generally increases across a period; decreases down a group [p. 22].  
  - **Electron‑gain enthalpy**: becomes more negative across a period; becomes less negative down a group [p. 22].  
  - **Electronegativity**: shows the same trend as ionization enthalpy (increase across a period, decrease down a group) [p. 22].

- **Metallic versus non‑metallic character** – Metallic character **increases down a group** and **decreases from left to right in a period**; non‑metallic character shows the opposite trend [p. 22].  
  - Metals make up >78 % of known elements; non‑metals are <20 %; elements at the border (e.g., Si, Ge, As) are metalloids [p. 22].

- **Valence behavior** – For representative elements, valence is either equal to the number of electrons in the outermost orbitals or eight minus that number [p. 22].

- **Chemical reactivity** – Highest at the two extremes of a period [p. 22].

These points highlight the principal differences emphasized in the classification‑of‑elements chapter. (All statements are taken directly from the provided context.)

**Q: How do the principles of classification-of-elements apply to problem solving?**

**A:** The principles of classification‑of‑elements—namely the Periodic Law, the basis of electronic configuration, the block (s, p, d, f) division, and the observed periodic trends—provide a framework that turns factual information into predictive tools for problem solving.  

- The Periodic Law states that when elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic weight (later atomic number), similar physical and chemical properties recur at regular intervals (p. 2). This allows one to anticipate the properties of an unknown element by locating its position in the table.  

- Electronic configuration is the underlying reason for the periodic repetition (p. 9). Knowing that the number of orbitals available for a given principal quantum number \(n\) determines how many elements can occupy a period enables quantitative problems, e.g., justifying why the 5th period contains 18 elements because for \(n=5\) the orbitals \(5s, 4d, 5p\) give a total of 9 orbitals and thus \(2\times9 = 18\) electrons (p. 9).  

- Classification into s‑, p‑, d‑ and f‑blocks groups elements with the same valence‑shell electron distribution, so members of a block share characteristic behaviours (p. 9). This block concept helps solve problems that ask for general trends (e.g., predicting that p‑block elements tend to form covalent compounds).  

- Periodic trends in properties such as metallic character, ionization enthalpy, and reactivity are direct consequences of the above principles. For instance, metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period from left to right (p. 12). Applying this rule, the order of increasing metallic character for the set Si, Be, Mg, Na, P is determined as P < Si < Be < Mg < Na (p. 12).  

Thus, by using the periodic law, electronic‑configuration‑based block classification, and the resulting periodic trends, one can solve problems ranging from predicting the number of elements in a period to arranging elements according to a specific property, all without memorising isolated facts. (pp. 1‑2, 9, 12)

**Q: State the important laws in classification-of-elements.**

**A:** The important laws in the classification of elements are:

- **Dobereiner’s Law of Triads** – states that in a triad of elements, the atomic weight of the middle element is approximately the average of the atomic weights of the other two, and its properties lie between those of the other two (Page 2).  
- **Newlands’ Law of Octaves** – states that when elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic weight, every eighth element shows properties similar to the first, analogous to musical octaves (Page 2).  
- **Mendeleev’s Periodic Law** – states that the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic weights (Page 2).  
- **Modern Periodic Law** – states that the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers (Page 5).  

These laws form the basis for the development of the periodic table and the understanding of periodicity in properties.

**Q: What are the SI units and dimensions of quantities in equilibrium?**

**A:** The SI unit for concentration used in Kc is mol L⁻¹ (which is equivalent to mol dm⁻³; in strict SI it is mol m⁻³).  
The SI unit for pressure used in Kp is the pascal (Pa) (1 Pa = 1 N m⁻²); the notes also note that the standard state for pressure is 1 bar (=10⁵ Pa), so pressures may be expressed in bar when calculating Kp.  

For a general reaction  

\[
aA+bB \rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

the equilibrium constant in concentration form is  

\[
K_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}
\]

and its unit is  

\[
[K_c]=\bigl(\text{mol L}^{-1}\bigr)^{\Delta n}\qquad\text{with }\Delta n=(c+d)-(a+b)
\]

(see Chapter Equilibrium, p. 13).  

Similarly, the pressure‑based constant is  

\[
K_p=\frac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}
\]

and its unit is  

\[
[K_p]=\bigl(\text{Pa}\bigr)^{\Delta n}\quad\text{(or }\bigl(\text{bar}\bigr)^{\Delta n}\text{ if pressure is expressed in bar)}
\]

(see Chapter Equilibrium, p. 11 and p. 13).  

If \(\Delta n =0\) (the total moles of gaseous products equal those of reactants), both Kc and Kp are dimensionless (no units).  

Thus, the SI units and dimensions of equilibrium‑constant quantities are derived from the units of concentration (mol L⁻¹) or pressure (Pa/bar) raised to the power \(\Delta n\).

**Q: What is the definition of the main concepts in equilibrium?**

**A:** The main concepts of equilibrium as presented in the notes are:

- **Dynamic equilibrium**: A state in which the opposing processes (e.g., evaporation and condensation, or forward and reverse reactions) occur simultaneously at equal rates, so that there is no net change in the concentrations (or amounts) of reactants and products, even though microscopic activity continues.  
  - *When the number of molecules leaving the liquid to vapour equals the number of molecules returning to the liquid from vapour, equilibrium is said to be attained and is dynamic in nature*【40†L1-L3】.  
  - *At this stage the rate of evaporation is equal to the rate of condensation*【1†L9-L10】.  
  - *When the reactants … give products … there is no change in the concentrations of either of the reactants or products. This stage … is the dynamic equilibrium and the rates of the forward and reverse reactions become equal*【1†L13-L16】.  
  - *this dynamic equilibrium stage that there is no change in the concentrations of various species in the reaction mixture*【2†L1-L2】.

- **Equilibrium constant (Kc)**: For a general reaction  
  \[
  aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD
  \]  
  the equilibrium constant is expressed as the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient:  
  \[
  K_c = \frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}
  \]  
  - *Equilibrium constant, Kc is expressed as the concentration of products divided by reactants, each term raised to the stoichiometric coefficient*【40†L5-L7】.  
  - The same expression is shown in the law of mass action: \(K_c \equiv \frac{[C][D]}{[A][B]}\) for the reaction \(A+B\rightleftharpoons C+D\)【8†L1-L4】.

- **Kp for gaseous reactions**: When dealing with gases, the equilibrium constant can be written in terms of partial pressures by replacing concentration terms in the Kc expression with the corresponding partial pressures.  
  - *For a gaseous reaction equilibrium constant is expressed as Kp and is written by replacing concentration terms by partial pressures in Kc expression*【40†L8-L9】.

- **Reaction quotient (Qc)**: Has the same form as Kc but uses the instantaneous concentrations (or partial pressures) of the reaction mixture; at equilibrium, \(Q_c = K_c\).  
  - *The direction of reaction can be predicted by reaction quotient Qc which is equal to Kc at equilibrium*【40†L9-L10】.

- **Law of mass action**: The principle that at equilibrium the concentrations of reactants and products are related by the equilibrium constant expression derived from experimental studies.  
  - *On the basis of experimental studies … Guldberg and Waage proposed … that the concentrations in an equilibrium mixture are related by the following equilibrium equation, \(K_c = \frac{[C][D]}{[A][B]}\)*【8†L1-L5】.  
  - *The equilibrium equation is also known as the law of mass action*【8†L6-L7】.

- **Le Chatelier’s principle**: If a factor (temperature, pressure, concentration, etc.) affecting an equilibrium system is changed, the equilibrium shifts in a direction that counteracts the change.  
  - *Le Chatelier’s principle states that the change in any factor such as temperature, pressure, concentration, etc. will cause the equilibrium to shift in such a direction so as to reduce or counteract the effect of the change*【40†L10-L13】.  
  - It can be used to study the effect of temperature, concentration, pressure, catalyst and inert gases on the equilibrium position and to control product yield【40†L13-L16】.

- **Effect of a catalyst**: A catalyst increases the rate of both forward and reverse reactions by providing a lower‑energy pathway, but it does not alter the equilibrium composition.  
  - *Catalyst does not effect the equilibrium composition of a reaction mixture but increases the rate of chemical reaction by making available a new lower energy pathway for conversion of reactants to products and vice‑versa*【40†L16-L19】.

**Q: Explain the physical significance of the concepts in equilibrium.**

**A:** The physical significance of equilibrium lies in its dynamic nature: opposing processes (such as evaporation and condensation, melting and freezing, or forward and reverse chemical reactions) occur simultaneously at equal rates, so there is no net change in the amounts of reactants and products or in macroscopic properties. This can be seen when a liquid evaporates in a closed container – the number of molecules leaving the liquid equals the number returning, giving a constant vapour pressure while molecules continually cross the interface (Page 1). For any physical process, equilibrium is attainable only in a closed system at a fixed temperature, and the system exhibits a dynamic but stable condition where both opposing processes proceed at the same rate (Page 5). Consequently, all measurable properties (e.g., pressure, concentration, temperature) remain constant, and the equilibrium is characterised by a constant value of one of its parameters (Page 5). The dynamic aspect can be demonstrated experimentally by continual exchange that produces no further change in observable levels, showing that the process continues even after macroscopic constancy (Page 6). In chemical reactions, the state of equilibrium is reached when the rates of the forward and reverse reactions become equal, leaving concentrations unchanged (Page 40). Thus, equilibrium signifies a balance where microscopic activity persists but macroscopic observables stay steady. (Pages 1, 2, 5, 6, 40)

**Q: State the important laws in thermodynamic.**

**A:** Based on the provided notes, the important laws of thermodynamics are:

1. **First Law of Thermodynamics:** 
   The energy of an isolated system is constant, commonly stated as the law of conservation of energy (energy can neither be created nor be destroyed). Mathematically, it is expressed as:
   $$\Delta U = q + w$$
   (Page 5)

2. **Second Law of Thermodynamics:** 
   For an isolated system, the change in energy remains constant, and the increase in entropy in such systems is the natural direction of a spontaneous change. It explains why spontaneous exothermic reactions are so common (because heat released increases the disorder of the surroundings, making the overall entropy change positive).
   (Page 26)

3. **Third Law of Thermodynamics:** 
   The entropy of any pure crystalline substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$). This is because there is perfect order in a crystal at absolute zero.
   (Page 26)

* **Denticity:** The number of ligating groups of a di- or polydentate ligand that simultaneously bind to a metal ion is called the denticity of the ligand (p. 4).
* **Ambidentate ligand:** A ligand which has two different donor atoms and either of the two ligates in the complex is called an ambidentate ligand (p. 4).
* **Coordination number (CN):** The coordination number of a metal ion in a complex can be defined as the number of ligand donor atoms to which the metal is directly bonded (determined only by the number of sigma bonds formed by the ligand with the central atom/ion) (pp. 4–5).
* **Coordination sphere:** The central atom/ion and the ligands attached to it are enclosed in square brackets and collectively termed the coordination sphere (p. 5).
* **Counter ions:** The ionisable groups written outside the bracket in a coordination compound are called counter ions (p. 5).
* **Coordination polyhedron:** The spatial arrangement of the ligand atoms which are directly attached to the central atom/ion defines a coordination polyhedron about the central atom (p. 5).
* **Oxidation number of central atom:** The oxidation number of the central atom in a complex is defined as the charge it would carry if all the ligands are removed along with the electron pairs that are shared with the central atom (p. 5).
* **Homoleptic complexes:** Complexes in which a metal is bound to only one kind of donor groups are known as homoleptic (p. 5).
* **Heteroleptic complexes:** Complexes in which a metal is bound to more than one kind of donor groups are known as heteroleptic (p. 5).

**Q: State the important laws in kinetic-theory.**

**A:** Based on the provided notes, the important laws and principles related to the kinetic theory and behaviour of gases mentioned are:

1. **Boyle’s Law**: For a given mass of gas at a constant temperature ($T$ and $\mu$ fixed), the pressure varies inversely with volume (Page 4):

   $$P = P_{1} + P_{2} + \dots$$

4. **Law of Equipartition of Energy**: If a system is in equilibrium at absolute temperature $T$, the total energy is distributed equally in different energy modes of absorption, with the energy in each mode being equal to $\frac{1}{2} k_{B} T$ (Page 13). Each translational and rotational degree of freedom has energy $\frac{1}{2} k_{B} T$, and each vibrational frequency has energy $2 \times \frac{1}{2} k_{B} T = k_{B} T$.

**Q: Derive the main equations in oscillations.**

**A:** Based on the provided notes, here are the derivations and expressions for the main equations in oscillations (Simple Harmonic Motion):

### 1. Displacement in Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)
The displacement $x(t)$ of a particle from its equilibrium position in SHM is given as (Page 14):
$$x(t) = A \cos (\omega t + \phi)$$
where $A$ is the amplitude, $(\omega t + \phi)$ is the phase, and $\phi$ is the phase constant.

### 2. Velocity and Acceleration in SHM
By differentiating the displacement equation with respect to time, the particle velocity $v(t)$ and acceleration $a(t)$ as functions of time are given by (Page 14):
$$v(t) = -\omega A \sin (\omega t + \phi)$$
$$a(t) = -\omega^2 A \cos (\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x(t)$$
The velocity amplitude is $v_m = \omega A$ and the acceleration amplitude is $a_m = \omega^2 A$ (Page 14).

### 3. Force Law for Simple Harmonic Motion
Using Newton’s second law of motion ($F = ma$) and the expression for acceleration in SHM ($a = -\omega^2 x$), the force acting on a particle of mass $m$ is (Page 9):
$$F(t) = -m\omega^2 x(t)$$
which can be written as:
$$F(t) = -k x(t)$$
where $k = m\omega^2$ is the spring constant, or angular frequency $\omega$ is given by (Page 9):
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

### 4. Period and Angular Frequency of a Linear Oscillator
A particle oscillating under Hooke's law restoring force ($F = -kx$) exhibits SHM with the following angular frequency and period (Page 14):
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$
$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

### 5. Simple Pendulum Time Period
For a simple pendulum swinging through small angles, the restoring torque is $\tau = -L(mg \sin \theta)$ (Page 13). Using rotational motion ($\tau = I\alpha$) and approximating $\sin \theta \approx \theta$ for small angles, the angular acceleration is (Page 13):
$$\alpha = -\frac{mgL}{I}\theta$$
Since the moment of inertia $I = mL^2$ for a simple pendulum, the angular frequency and time period are given by (Page 13):
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgL}{I}}$$
$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{I}{mgL}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$$

**Q: What is the definition of the main concepts in properties-of-solids?**

**A:** Based on the provided notes, the main concepts related to the properties of solids are defined as follows:

1. **Elasticity**: The property of a body, by virtue of which it tends to regain its original size and shape when the applied force is removed (Page 1).
2. **Plasticity**: The property by virtue of which substances have no gross tendency to regain their previous shape and get permanently deformed when force is applied (Page 1).
3. **Stress**: Stress is defined as the internal restoring force per unit area (or applied force per unit area for hydraulic stress) (Pages 1, 3, 10).

**1. Derivation of Eq. (6.36): $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$**

$$\frac{d\omega}{dt} = \alpha$$ (constant)

Integrating this equation with respect to time $t$:
$$\int d\omega = \int \alpha \, dt$$
$$\omega = \alpha t + c$$ (where $c$ is a constant of integration)

$$\omega_0 = \alpha(0) + c \implies c = \omega_0$$

Thus, we obtain the required equation:
$$\omega = \alpha t + \omega_0$$

**Q: Explain the physical significance of the concepts in Units and Measurements.**

**A:** The physical significance of the concepts in Units and Measurements lies in providing a common language for comparing and communicating physical quantities, ensuring that measurements are meaningful, precise, and universally understandable.  

- A measurement expresses a physical quantity as a number accompanied by a unit, which is an internationally accepted reference standard; this allows any result to be compared with others (Page 1).  
- Because many physical quantities are inter‑related, only a limited set of base units (for length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity) is needed; all other quantities can be expressed as derived units formed by combining these base units (Page 1, Page 10 points 2 & 3).  
- A complete set of base and derived units constitutes a system of units; the SI system, based on seven base units, is the internationally accepted standard, ensuring uniformity in scientific and technical work worldwide (Page 10 points 4 & 5).  
- SI units have well‑defined symbols (e.g., m, kg, s, A, N) that eliminate ambiguity in notation (Page 10 point 6).  
- Scientific notation and SI prefixes simplify the expression of very large or very small numbers, make numerical computation easier, and give an indication of the precision of the measurement (Page 10 point 7).  
- Proper use of symbols, units, and prefixes follows specific rules that guarantee clarity and consistency in reporting measurements (Page 10 point 8).  
- In calculations, units can be treated as algebraic quantities; they cancel or combine just like numbers, which helps obtain the correct final units (Page 10 point 9).  
- Significant figures convey the reliability of a measurement: they include all certain digits plus the first uncertain digit, reflecting the precision limited by the least count of the instrument (Page 3). Reporting more digits than significant figures would misrepresent the precision (Page 3).  
- To avoid rounding‑off errors in multi‑step calculations, it is advisable to retain one extra digit in intermediate steps (Page 7).  
- Dimensions describe the nature of a physical quantity by showing how the base quantities (mass [M], length [L], time [T], etc.) are combined; for example, volume has dimensions [L]³ and force has dimensions [M L T⁻²] (Page 7).  
- Dimensional analysis—checking that the dimensions on both sides of an equation match—can detect dimensional inconsistencies (which indicate an incorrect equation) and can help deduce relations among physical quantities (Page 10 point 11, Page 7).  

Together, these concepts ensure that measurements are accurate, comparable, and meaningful across different contexts and disciplines. (Pages 1, 3, 7, 10, 11)

**Q: What is the definition of the main concepts in Units and Measurements?**

**A:** **Measurement** – “Measurement of any physical quantity involves comparison with a certain basic, arbitrarily chosen, internationally accepted reference standard called unit. The result of a measurement of a physical quantity is expressed by a number (or numerical measure) accompanied by a unit.” [p.1]  

**Unit** – “A certain basic, arbitrarily chosen but properly standardised reference standard” used for comparison; e.g., metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela. [p.1], [p.10]  

**Base (fundamental) quantities** – The seven quantities chosen as fundamental: length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity. [p.10]

**Derived units** – Units of derived quantities, expressed as combinations of base units (e.g., newton, joule, watt). [p.1], [p.10]

- Example: 1.62 s → three significant figures; 287.5 cm → four significant figures. [p.3]

**Q: What are the important differences between concepts in Units and Measurements?**

**A:** The notes highlight several key distinctions among the concepts introduced in the chapter “Units and Measurement”:

- **Base quantities vs. derived quantities** – Base quantities (length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity) are chosen as fundamental; all other physical quantities are derived from these base quantities (page 1, page 10 summary point 1‑2).  
- **Base units vs. derived units** – Units for base quantities are called base units (metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela); units for derived quantities are obtained by combining base units and are called derived units (page 1, page 10 summary point 3).  
- **Unit vs. measurement** – A unit is an internationally accepted reference standard; a measurement expresses a physical quantity as a number accompanied by that unit (page 1).  
- **Significant figures vs. dimensions** – Significant figures indicate the precision of a measurement (reliable digits plus the first uncertain digit) and depend on the least count of the instrument; changing units does not alter their number (page 3). Dimensions describe the nature of a physical quantity (combination of base‑quantity dimensions) and are used in dimensional analysis to check equation consistency and deduce relations (page 1, page 10 summary point 11).  
- **System of units vs. individual units** – A system of units is a complete set containing both base and derived units (e.g., SI, CGS, FPS, MKS); individual units are the specific standards within that system (page 1, page 10 summary point 3‑4).  
- **SI versus other systems** – The SI is the internationally accepted system based on seven base units; earlier systems such as CGS (centimetre‑gram‑second), FPS (foot‑pound‑second) and MKS (metre‑kilogram‑second) used different base units for length, mass and time (page 1).  
- **Scientific notation vs. ordinary notation** – For very large or small measurements, scientific notation (powers of 10) together with SI prefixes simplifies notation and computation, whereas ordinary notation does not provide this convenience (page 10 summary point 7).  
- **Rules for symbols and prefixes** – Specific guidelines govern the use of symbols for physical quantities, chemical elements, nuclides and SI units/prefixes to ensure proper expression (page 10 summary point 8).  
- **Algebraic treatment of units** – In calculations, units of derived quantities are manipulated algebraically until the desired unit is obtained (page 10 summary point 9).  
- **Retention of significant figures in results** – After computation, only the appropriate number of significant figures should be kept, following rules for arithmetic operations and rounding (page 10 summary point 10).  

These points capture the important differences between the various concepts discussed in the notes.

**Q: How do the principles of Units and Measurements apply to problem solving?**

**A:** The principles of units and measurements help solve physics problems by ensuring that every step of a calculation is dimensionally sound and that the final answer is expressed in correct units.  

1. **Dimensional consistency (principle of homogeneity)** – Only quantities with the same dimensions can be added, subtracted, or equated. If an equation’s terms do not all reduce to the same dimension, the equation is wrong (p. 8). This lets you check a derived formula before doing any algebra.  

2. **Checking each term’s dimensions** – Write the dimension of every symbol using the base‑quantity symbols \([L], [M], [T]\) etc. (p. 7‑8). For example, in the expression for distance  
   \[
   x = x_{0}+v_{0}t+\tfrac12at^{2},
   \]  
   the dimensions are \([x]=[L]\), \([x_{0}]=[L]\), \([v_{0}t]=[L T^{-1}][T]=[L]\), and \([\tfrac12at^{2}]=[L T^{-2}][T^{2}]=[L]\) (p. 8). All terms reduce to \([L]\), confirming the equation is dimensionally consistent.  

3. **Units as algebraic symbols** – When magnitudes are multiplied, their units obey the same rules as ordinary algebraic symbols; identical units in numerator and denominator cancel (p. 8). This guides unit conversion and simplification during problem solving.  

4. **Dimensionless arguments of functions** – The arguments of trigonometric, logarithmic, exponential, etc., must be dimensionless (p. 8). If a problem involves such a function, you must first combine quantities to form a dimensionless ratio (e.g., an angle as length/length).  

5. **Deriving relations via dimensional analysis** – By equating dimensions on both sides of an assumed relationship, you can deduce how physical quantities depend on one another (p. 8). This is useful when the exact form of a law is unknown.  

6. **Expressing quantities in a chosen unit system** – When a new set of base units is defined (mass = α kg, length = β m, time = γ s), the magnitude of a derived quantity changes according to the powers of α, β, γ (p. 11). This shows how to convert results between unit systems.  

7. **Using significant figures and rounding rules** – Retain only proper significant figures in measured and computed quantities; keep an extra digit in intermediate steps to avoid rounding errors (p. 7).  

By applying these steps—checking dimensions, treating units algebraically, ensuring dimensionless function arguments, and converting units correctly—you can set up, manipulate, and verify equations confidently, leading to reliable problem‑solving outcomes.  

(Citations: p. 7, p. 8, p. 11)

**Q: What are the limitations of the laws in current-electricity?**

**A:** Although Ohm's law has been found valid over a large class of materials, there do exist materials and devices used in electric circuits where the proportionality of $V$ and $I$ does not hold. The deviations broadly are one or more of the following types (Page 89):

(a) $V$ ceases to be proportional to $I$ (Fig. 3.5).
(b) The relation between $V$ and $I$ depends on the sign of $V$. In other words, if $I$ is the current for a certain $V$, then reversing the direction of $V$ keeping its magnitude fixed, does not produce a current of the same magnitude as $I$ in the opposite direction (Fig. 3.6). This happens, for example, in a diode.
(c) The relation between $V$ and $I$ is not unique, i.e., there is more than one value of $V$ for the same current $I$ (Fig. 3.7). A material exhibiting such behaviour is GaAs.

Additionally, Ohm's law is not a fundamental law of nature and fails if (Page 103):
(a) $V$ depends on $I$ non-linearly.
(b) The relation between $V$ and $I$ depends on the sign of $V$ for the same absolute value of $V$.
(c) The relation between $V$ and $I$ is non-unique.

**Q: How do the principles of current-electricity apply to problem solving?**

**A:** Based on the provided notes (pages 18 to 22), the principles of current-electricity—specifically Kirchhoff's rules, Ohm's law, and the Wheatstone bridge balance condition—are applied to problem-solving in the following ways:

1. **Kirchhoff's First Rule (Junction Rule):** The total current flowing into a junction must equal the total current flowing out, because there is no accumulation of charges at any junction when currents are steady (Page 18). This is used to assign and relate unknown currents at junctions (e.g., $I_{3} = I_{1} + I_{2}$) to reduce the number of unknowns in a network (Pages 19, 20).

2. **Kirchhoff's Second Rule (Loop Rule):** The algebraic sum of changes in potential around any closed loop involving resistors and cells is zero (Page 18):
$$\sum \Delta V = 0$$
This is applied to various closed loops in a circuit to generate simultaneous equations for unknown currents, which can then be solved (Pages 19, 20, 22). Symmetry can also be exploited to simplify networks like a cubical resistor array (Page 19).

3. **Ohm's Law:** The electric current $I$ flowing through a substance is proportional to the voltage $V$ across its ends:
$$V = RI$$
where $R$ is the resistance (Page 22). It is used alongside circuit rules to relate potentials, currents, and resistances.

4. **Wheatstone Bridge Balance Condition:** For a bridge with four resistors $R_{1}$, $R_{2}$, $R_{3}$, and $R_{4}$ where the galvanometer shows zero or null deflection ($I_{g} = 0$), the balance condition is given by (Page 21):
$$\frac{R_{2}}{R_{4}} = \frac{R_{1}}{R_{3}}$$
or 
$$\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}} = \frac{R_{4}}{R_{3}}$$
This principle provides a practical method to determine an unknown resistance (such as $R_{4}$) by varying a known resistance until the bridge is balanced (Page 21).

   - **Extrinsic semiconductors:** Semiconductors where the number of charge carriers is changed by 'doping' a suitable impurity. They are of two types: n-type and p-type (Page 18).

2. **n-type vs. p-type Semiconductors:**
   - **n-type semiconductors:** Electrons are the majority carriers ($n_e \gg n_h$). They are obtained by doping Si or Ge with pentavalent atoms (donors) like As, Sb, P, etc. (Page 18, Exercise 14.1).
   - **p-type semiconductors:** Holes are the majority carriers ($n_h \gg n_e$). They are obtained by doping with trivalent atoms (acceptors) like B, Al, In, etc. (Page 18, Exercise 14.1, Exercise 14.2).
   - For all cases, $n_e n_h = n_i^2$, and the material possesses overall charge neutrality (Page 18).

3. **Classification based on Energy Bands (Insulators, Semiconductors, and Metals):**
   - **Insulators:** Energy gap $E_g > 3\text{ eV}$ (Page 19).
   - **Semiconductors:** Energy gap $E_g$ is $0.2\text{ eV}$ to $3\text{ eV}$ (Page 19).
   - **Metals:** Energy gap $E_g \approx 0$ (Page 19).
   - **Valence Band vs. Conduction Band:** Valence band energies are low compared to conduction band energies. Valence band energy levels are all filled, while conduction band energy levels may be fully empty or partially filled. Electrons in the conduction band are free to move and are responsible for conductivity, depending upon the energy gap ($E_g$) between the top of the valence band ($E_V$) and the bottom of the conduction band ($E_C$) (Page 18).

4. **Types of Semiconductors by Material:**
   - **Elemental semiconductors:** Si and Ge (Pages 2-3).
   - **Compound semiconductors:** Inorganic (e.g., CdS, GaAs, CdSe, InP), organic (e.g., anthracene, doped pthalocyanines), and organic polymers (e.g., polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene) (Pages 2-3).

**Q: Explain the physical significance of the concepts in semiconductors.**

**A:** Based on the provided notes, the physical significance of the concepts in semiconductors includes:

1. **Intrinsic Semiconductors and Charge Carriers:** In pure (intrinsic) semiconductors, thermal excitation provides energy that breaks covalent bonds, creating free electrons (with a charge of $-q$) and vacancies known as **holes** (which behave as apparent free particles with an effective positive charge) (Pages 5, 18). The number of free electrons ($n_e$) equals the number of holes ($n_h$), such that $n_e = n_h = n_i$, where $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier concentration (Page 5). Both electrons and holes move and contribute to electrical conduction (Pages 3, 5, 18).

2. **Energy Bands and Conductivity:** Materials contain a valence band (low energy, filled with valence electrons) and a conduction band (higher energy, responsible for conductivity) separated by an energy gap $E_g$ (Pages 3, 18):
   - In semiconductors, there is a finite but small band gap ($E_g < 3\text{ eV}$). At room temperature, some electrons acquire enough thermal energy to cross into the conduction band, giving semiconductors an intermediate resistance (higher than metals, but much lower than insulators) (Page 5).

3. **Extrinsic Semiconductors and Doping:** The number of charge carriers can be altered by "doping" pure semiconductors with suitable impurities to create extrinsic semiconductors (Page 18):
   - **n-type semiconductors:** Obtained by doping with pentavalent atoms (donors) like As, Sb, or P, resulting in $n_e \gg n_h$ (Page 18). Donor energy levels ($E_D$) lie slightly below the bottom of the conduction band ($E_C$), allowing electrons to move into the conduction band with a very small supply of energy (Page 9).
   - **p-type semiconductors:** Obtained by doping with trivalent atoms (acceptors) like B, Al, or In, resulting in $n_h \gg n_e$ (Page 18).
   - In all cases, the relation $n_e n_h = n_i^2$ holds, and the material maintains an overall charge neutrality (Page 18).

The branch of optics where the finiteness of the wavelength is completely neglected is called geometrical optics, and a ray is defined as the path of energy propagation in the limit of wavelength tending to zero.

* **Plane wave:** At a large distance from the source, a small portion of the spherical wave can be considered as a plane wave (Page 3).
* **Geometrical optics:** The branch of optics in which one completely neglects the finiteness of the wavelength (Page 2).
* **Ray:** Defined as the path of energy propagation in the limit of wavelength tending to zero (Page 2).
